What is a K=3 Wang cube?

A precise account of one small, symbolic corner of the three-dimensional einstein problem — for readers comfortable with groups and lattices but new to tiling theory.

1The problem it belongs to

Call a shape a space-filler if congruent copies of it can be packed to fill \(\mathbb{R}^n\) with no gaps or overlaps. A tiling is periodic if it is left unchanged by some full-rank lattice of translations — informally, it has a repeating fundamental block that recurs in \(n\) independent directions.

Almost every space-filler you can picture also admits a periodic packing. The subtle question, open for decades, is whether a single tile can be forced to be aperiodic:

The einstein problem. Does there exist one tile that tiles space, but such that no tiling of it is periodic? (German ein Stein, "one stone.")

In the plane this was open until 2023, when the "hat" and "spectre" tiles settled it: yes. In three dimensions it is open. The object below is a deliberately restricted, fully combinatorial 3-D family in which the question becomes finite and decidable — and, as it turns out, answerable.

2Wang tiles: turning geometry into matching rules

The classical device (Hao Wang, 1961) replaces geometry with edge-matching. A Wang tile is a unit square whose four edges carry labels; copies may be placed edge-to-edge exactly when touching edges agree. This converts "does this tile fill space?" into a purely local, symbolic constraint-satisfaction problem — which is what makes such tiles amenable to exhaustive search and to proof.

Our object is the three-dimensional analogue, with two deliberate design choices that keep it finite and physical:

  • A cube in place of a square, so the six faces carry the matching data.
  • Bump/dent relief in place of colors. Each face carries a small grid of raised bumps and sunken dents; two faces interlock exactly when they are complementary — every bump meets a dent and every dent meets a bump. (This is why a face matches itself only through its mirror partner — the natural rule for a physical interlocking block.)

3The K=3 cube, precisely

Fix a resolution \(K\). Each of the cube's 6 faces carries a \(K\times K\) grid, and each grid cell is either a bump or a dent — one bit. At \(K=3\):

The tile. A decoration is a function \(\mathrm{dec}:\{0,1,\dots,53\}\to\{\text{bump},\text{dent}\}\), one bit for each of the \(6\times 3\times 3 = 54\) face cells. A single decoration is the tile. There are \(2^{54}\approx 1.8\times 10^{16}\) of them.

Two further ingredients complete the model.

Orientations. A cube has a rotation group of order 24 (isomorphic to \(S_4\)). A copy of the tile may be placed in any of these 24 rotations, which permutes where its 54 decorated cells point.
Tiling \(\mathbb{Z}^3\). An orientation field \(o:\mathbb{Z}^3\to\{24\text{ rotations}\}\) is a tiling by the decoration \(\mathrm{dec}\) if, for every pair of adjacent cells, the two faces meeting across their shared boundary — the same \(\mathrm{dec}\), read through the two chosen rotations — are complementary.

Note what is and isn't free: there is one fixed tile (the 54 bits), and the only freedom in building a tiling is choosing a rotation for each lattice cell. That is exactly the "single tile" spirit of the einstein problem.

4Periodic, aperiodic, einstein

An orientation field \(o\) is periodic if it is invariant under a full-rank sublattice \(L\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^3\): there are three independent translations \(\ell\) with \(o(p+\ell)=o(p)\) for all \(p\). The tile is an aperiodic einstein if it tiles \(\mathbb{Z}^3\) but no tiling of it is periodic in this sense. The precise question:

The K=3 question. Is there a decoration \(\mathrm{dec}\in\{0,1\}^{54}\) that tiles \(\mathbb{Z}^3\), yet admits no periodic tiling?

Because everything is finite and local, this is decidable — unlike the general 3-D einstein problem, this restricted version can, in principle, be settled by computation. It was.

5The answer

Theorem (machine-checked). No. Every \(K=3\) Wang cube that tiles \(\mathbb{Z}^3\) admits a fully periodic tiling — in fact one with a fundamental domain of at most 864 cells. There is no aperiodic einstein in this family.

The proof reduces the geometric statement, through a chain of formally verified steps, to a single Boolean unsatisfiability claim about a formula with 785,686 clauses, which was discharged by splitting it into 1457 sub-problems and having each machine-checked by a formally verified SAT-proof checker. Crucially the SAT solver itself is not trusted — only its certificates, re-checked independently. So the result rests on the logical kernel plus that one external, re-verified input, and not on the correctness of any search program.

6Why it is true: you can't screw irrationally with a finite group

The verification is more than a brute-force "we checked everything": the configurations it rules out have a clean shape. Every way a \(K=3\) cube can tile turns out to contain a screw — along some direction the cube rotates as it translates, cycling through a sequence of orientations before the pattern repeats.

Here is the punchline. The cube's orientations form a finite group of order 24. So any screw the tiling attempts must close: after finitely many steps the accumulated rotation returns to the identity — and a closed screw is exactly a translation period. A finite rotation group has no room for a screw that never repeats. In one sentence:

You cannot build an irrational screw out of a finite group. The cube tiles by screws; its screws all close because 24 rotations is finite; a closed screw is a period; so every tiling is periodic.

This is precisely the loophole a genuine 3-D einstein would need. The Schmitt–Conway–Danzer tile — the closest classical object — forces aperiodicity through a screw by an irrational angle, which never closes. A symbolic cube, confined to the rational rotations of a finite group, can only screw in ways that eventually come home. That finiteness is the whole obstruction.

7What this does and doesn't settle

This resolves one honest, well-defined family: single Wang cubes at face-resolution \(K=3\), with binary bump/dent matching and the 24-rotation orientation group, tiling \(\mathbb{Z}^3\). Refinement (copying each bit into a finer block) shows \(K=3\) already subsumes \(K=1\); the same machinery applies to other \(K\) and other matching rules, and the screw argument suggests the answer stays "no" wherever the orientation group is finite. It does not touch the general 3-D einstein problem, where the tile is an arbitrary solid and the symmetry group is the full continuous group of rigid motions — there, an irrational screw is back on the table, and the question remains open.
