This is a genuinely nice result, and the part you should be proudest of is exactly the part you flagged: the semantic bridge. The graveyard of "we searched and found nothing" results in tiling theory is large, and the standard failure mode is precisely the unexamined claim that a finite window + a pattern criterion captures the infinite problem. Having L2 kernel-checked — each forbidden pattern carrying an explicit torus certificate, transported across the orientation group — is what makes this a theorem rather than an experiment. And note the theorem is actually in the *strongest* negative form: not just "no aperiodic cube exists" but "every space-tiler admits a fully periodic tiling," which simultaneously kills weak and strong aperiodicity (the distinction that matters for things like the Schmitt–Conway–Danzer tile is moot here).

A few thoughts, then directions.

### Hardening and sharpening the current result

Two audit questions worth answering before you write this up. First, the symmetry reduction uses the full 48-element group but the bridge transports certificates across the 24 rotations — make sure the pattern set (or the bridge) is genuinely closed under the improper symmetries too, since a reflection conjugates a tiling's orientation assignment nontrivially. If `PInv*` already handles this, say so loudly in the paper, because it's the first place a referee will poke. Second, consider replacing the `ConcreteTree.perCubeUnsat` axiom by internalizing the LRAT checking via a Lean-verified checker (the LeanSAT/Trestle line of work has one usable with `native_decide`). That would collapse your trust base to standard axioms + `native_decide` only, with no bespoke axiom at all — arguably cleaner than the Keller/Schur-5 setup you're currently matching.

There are also cheap quantitative theorems sitting in your artifacts. The 33 torus certificates each have a period; take the max and you get a crisp statement like "every K=3 cube that tiles ℤ³ admits a periodic tiling with fundamental domain at most N³." Similarly: is 4³ the minimal window (does 3³ fail)? Are all 33 patterns necessary (drop-one experiments / UNSAT core minimization)? A minimal certificate is both more elegant and more likely to be human-interpretable.

### The K-lattice, and a free win

Here's a structural observation you should exploit: aperiodicity is monotone under face refinement. A K-decoration lifts to an mK-decoration by copying each cell's bit into an m×m block, and this preserves matching semantics in both directions, so "no aperiodic cube at K=mK′" implies "no aperiodic cube at K′". Your K=3 result therefore already covers K=1. K=2 is only 24 bits — your pipeline should eat it for breakfast — and then everything with K ∈ {1,2,3} is settled and K=4 (96 bits) becomes the clean frontier. K=6 would subsume 2 and 3 but is presumably hopeless by brute force. The monotonicity also frames the big question nicely: either there's a threshold K₀ where aperiodic cubes appear and persist forever, or they never exist at any resolution. Even a heuristic argument for one side of that dichotomy would be interesting.

### The orbit-constraint theory (my favorite direction)

A Wang cube under 24 rotations is, in effect, a set of ≤24 Wang cubes with the very strong constraint that they form a single symmetry orbit. Culik–Kari-style aperiodic Wang cube *sets* exist in 3D with ~20 unconstrained cubes; Jeandel–Rao showed 11 tiles suffice in 2D. So the einstein question in your family is really: **can an aperiodic Wang cube set fit inside one rotation orbit?** This suggests both an attack and a construction program. Attack: characterize which abstract 24-tile-sets-with-orbit-structure are realizable at a given K, and look for a symmetry obstruction that works for *all* K — that would be a hand-provable theorem strictly stronger than any SAT sweep. Construction: try to embed a known small aperiodic 2D Wang set into horizontal layers, using the vertical faces to force layer alignment and the rotation orbit to generate the tile variety. If the embedding fails, the failure mode is data for the obstruction; if it succeeds at some K, you've built a 3D symbolic einstein.

### Nearby dials

The pipeline generalizes along several axes, and mapping the phase diagram would be a real contribution: number of tiles (what's the minimal number of K-cubes for an aperiodic *set* — the 3D Jeandel–Rao program, and "no aperiodic pair at K=2" is probably in reach right now), alphabet size (colored faces with equality matching rather than binary bump/dent), and orientation group (the order-12 tetrahedral subgroup, or adding reflections to get 48). Somewhere in that parameter space aperiodicity switches on — Culik–Kari guarantees it in the multi-tile direction — and locating the frontier with machine-checked negatives on one side and explicit constructions on the other would be a lovely paper series.

### Wilder

Undecidability: is the tiling problem for a single K-cube-with-rotations undecidable for large enough K? A single tile plus a finite symmetry group is an extremely constrained input format, in the spirit of Greenfeld–Tao's single-translational-tile results. If yes, that would also explain *why* the einstein question here is hard and put a ceiling on how far the SAT program can go; if the problem is decidable for every fixed K, that itself would be surprising.

Insight extraction: the 33 patterns are a machine-found proof skeleton. Classify them — are they instances of a few families like "this local configuration admits a flip that closes up a torus"? If they compress to two or three human-legible lemmas ("any K=3 tiling admits a local defect-repair move that forces periodicity"), you'd have the increasingly rare artifact of a machine proof becoming a human theorem. A companion "atlas" is also cheap: enumerate all K=3 tilers up to symmetry and their minimal periods — a complete structure theory for the family, not just the einstein question.

Physical spin: 3×3 binary bump/dent faces is essentially the geometry of DNA bricks and interlocking-block self-assembly. Your theorem says any space-filling assembly at that interface resolution can relax to a crystal — so aperiodic self-assembled materials in this scheme *require* K≥4 or richer glues. That reframing makes a K=4 *positive* search (ML-guided or CEGIS-hybrid, hunting for candidates that survive deep period-finding rather than proving exhaustive negatives) interesting to a totally different audience.

Finally, package `CubeCover` and the bridge/symmetry-reduction pattern as a standalone library. "SAT search → kernel-checked theorem" pipelines for tiling problems are currently rebuilt from scratch every time (Keller, packing chromatic, etc.); a reusable template might be the most-cited thing to come out of this.

If I had to pick two: do K=2 immediately (it's nearly free and completes the small-K picture), and put serious thought into the orbit-constraint obstruction — it's the only path I see to a statement covering all K, which is the theorem everyone actually wants.
