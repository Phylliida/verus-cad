Erdős, 1946 — refuted, 2026

How many pairs of n points in the plane
can be exactly one apart?

For 80 years, mathematicians believed the answer was at most n1+o(1). In May 2026, an internal model at OpenAI produced a counterexample. The proof passes through an infinite tower of number fields, a Galois cohomology argument, and a high-dimensional lattice projection. The savings over linear is real, infinitesimal, and beautiful.

The problem

Pick n distinct points in the Euclidean plane. Count the unordered pairs at distance exactly 1. Call that count ν(P), and let ν(n) be its maximum.

Three classical facts pin down ν(n):

  • Two unit circles meet in at most 2 points, so the unit-distance graph is K2,3-free. Kővári–Sós–Turán gives ν(n) = O(n3/2).
  • Spencer, Szemerédi, Trotter (1984) sharpened this to ν(n) = O(n4/3) via a crossing-lemma argument. This bound has stood for 42 years.
  • Erdős's √n × √n grid achieves ν(n) ≥ n1+c/log log n. This was the best known lower bound.

Erdős conjectured the upper bound was tight: ν(n) ≤ n1+O(1/log log n). The new theorem refutes this.

Erdős's grid construction

The classical lower bound uses Gaussian integers ℤ[i]. Pick a radius m with many representations as a sum of two squares — concretely, take m = p1·p2···pk, a product of primes ≡ 1 (mod 4). The number of representations r2(m) = 4·2k. After rescaling so the radius is 1, each grid point has roughly 2k unit-distance neighbors. Optimizing k ~ log n / log log n yields the famous exponent.

This construction was, until 2026, believed essentially optimal. The conjecture ν(n) ≤ n1+O(1/log log n) said: no matter how clever you are, you cannot do meaningfully better than counting Gaussian integers on a circle. The new construction breaks this principle by working in many number fields at once.

Why number fields?

Erdős's grid is, secretly, an arithmetic object. The Gaussian integers ℤ[i] are the ring of integers of ℚ(i), a degree-2 CM field. Unit vectors come from elements u ∈ ℚ(i) with |u| = 1 — equivalently, u·ū = 1.

The natural generalization is to fix the degree of the field but vary the number of split primes. Every approach in this direction reproduces the same divisor-function bound. The AI's move is the opposite: fix the primes, grow the degree. Replace ℚ(i) by K = L(i) for a totally real field L of degree d → ∞.

"…in principle all extremal examples can be taken algebraic. But the degree and height of that algebraic realization can be enormous… Maybe that enormous degree is not just an annoyance but a source of possible counterexamples. Number fields deserve a closer look." — the model's chain of thought

Step 1–3: a tower with prescribed splitting

Take ℓ distinct rational primes ri ≡ 1 (mod 3) and let F be the cyclic cubic subfield of the compositum cut out by χ1···χℓ. The conductor–discriminant formula forces M/F to be everywhere unramified, where M is the full compositum. This gives the generator rank d(G) ≥ ℓ − 1 for the maximal unramified pro-3 group G.

Golod–Shafarevich now kicks in: as long as r(G) ≤ d(G)²/4, the group is infinite. The base has so much slack that you can also kill the Frobenius elements at t ≈ d²/100 chosen rational primes — forcing them to split completely in the entire tower — without breaking infinitude. The primes themselves are chosen by Chebotarev so their Frobenii sit in the Frattini subgroup Φ(G); killing such elements doesn't reduce the generator rank.

Step 4: the CM trick

For each Fj in the tower, set Kj = Fj(i). Since Fj is totally real, Kj is a CM field with complex conjugation c as its nontrivial automorphism. The key identity:

For every complex embedding σ and every α ∈ Kj×:   σ(c(α)) = σ(α)

So if we set u = α / c(α), every complex absolute value of u equals 1 — automatically. This is Hilbert 90 for the CM involution. One algebraic computation gives unit-modulus in every embedding simultaneously. (Compare: a random algebraic unit has |σ(u)| = 1 in one embedding only on a measure-zero set.)

Step 5: sign ideals, pigeonhole, projection

Each split prime qb contributes fj = [Fj:ℚ] conjugate prime pairs (𝔓s, c𝔓s) in Kj. For each binary sign vector ε ∈ {0,1}tfj, form an ideal 𝔄ε by picking one prime from each pair according to ε. That's 2tfj ideals — but they live in the class group Cl(Kj), which has size ≤ Hfj.

The negative resolution requires t log 2 > log H. When this holds, the number of unit directions is exponential in fj — and exponential is what defeats n1+o(1).

For each ε in a common class fibre, choose αε with (αε) = 𝔄ε𝔄η−1 and set uε = αε/c(αε). The valuations of uε at the chosen primes are 2(εs − ηs) ∈ {−2, 0, 2} — so different ε give different uε, and all of them live in the fixed lattice Q−2𝒪Kj where Q = ∏ qb.

Now embed Kj ↪ ℂfj (one embedding from each conjugate pair) and intersect with a polydisc of radius R. By averaging over translates of the lattice, some coset has many (x, x+u) pairs — and every coordinate of u has modulus 1, so the overlap volume is geometric. Project to the first coordinate. Distinct lattice points project to distinct planar points (a field embedding is injective). Each counted pair becomes a planar unit segment. Done.

The better grid, rendered

The smallest non-trivial instance is K = ℚ(ζ8) = ℚ(i, √2), a CM field of degree 4 with totally real subfield F = ℚ(√2). There are two pairs of complex embeddings; pick one from each, giving a Minkowski embedding K ↪ ℂ2. The ring of integers ℤ[ζ8] becomes a rank-4 lattice in ℂ2.

The "visible" plane is the first complex coordinate σ1. The lattice projected to σ1 alone is dense. The cut comes from constraining the hidden coordinate σ2 to a disk. The eight roots of unity ζ8k sit in ℤ[ζ8] and have modulus 1 in both embeddings — these are the unit-distance directions. (Erdős's ℤ[i] grid has only four: ±1, ±i.)

Shrink Rh and the cut becomes selective: fewer lattice points survive into the visible plane, but those that do are arranged with the octagonal symmetry inherited from Gal(ℚ(ζ8)/ℚ). Each interior vertex has up to eight unit-distance neighbors — twice the four of Erdős's square grid. This is the "improvement" at degree 4: the count of unit directions tracks the size of the unit group in 𝒪K.

To get the asymptotic n1+δ rather than just a doubled constant, you need more directions than roots of unity provide. The full construction adds S-units from marked split primes: for each rational prime qb ≡ 1 (mod 8) that splits completely in the tower, you get ~ 2tfj/h(Kj) additional directions of the form α/c̄(α) with denominator dividing qb2. At [F:ℚ] = 2 the smallest such prime is 17 and the gain is modest; the real win compounds as fj → ∞ along the pro-3 tower over the cubic base field.

Inside the model's thinking

The chain of thought rules out the natural approaches one by one before finding the working idea. A condensed reconstruction:

Stage 1 — Skepticism "A loophole, if there is one, would have to lie in the quantifiers or in the formulation… The standard lattice construction only gives one fixed constant in the numerator, not arbitrarily large constants."
Stage 2 — Failed amplifications Hypercubes give only n log n. Roots of unity give log n · log log log n. Powers of a single algebraic number give logarithmically many directions for exponential coefficient growth. None of these easy constructions produces the arbitrarily large constant in the 1 + C/log log n exponent.
Stage 3 — Number-field intuition "Maybe that enormous degree is not just an annoyance but a source of possible counterexamples." The model considers Salem numbers, almost-totally-real fields, mixed-signature quadratic extensions, then the CM trick.
Stage 4 — The mechanism "Suppose optimistically that K is a high-degree CM field in which a fixed rational prime p splits completely into principal prime ideals. Then the construction is frightening." This is the moment the model realizes the construction works. It then spends many pages verifying the class-number bound, the Golod–Shafarevich step, the packing estimate, and the projection.

What the model never does: introduce a fundamentally new tool. The components — Golod–Shafarevich towers with prescribed splitting (Hajir–Maire–Ramakrishna), unramified class field towers, CM fields, geometry of numbers — have all been on the shelf for decades. The novelty is the assembly.

Coda

The new lower bound is microscopic — δ ≈ 6.24 × 10−38 is small enough that no telescope can see it. But it is positive and absolute, and that is enough. The conjecture said no constant works; the construction provides one. Erdős's $500 problem is solved.

What's left: distinct distances, the unit-distance problem in ℝ³, and the entire program of asking which other conjectures in discrete geometry hide arithmetic constructions inside them. Sawin's commentary lays out exactly why these adjacent problems are harder. The remarks paper closes:
